In spite of the fact that in a historical per spective leisure time for labour force partici pants has increased, life seems generally mo re hectic than before. Enterprises and indivi duals often go to great expense to save time. Economic theories of time utilisation ex plain this paradox as follows: Economic growth leads to steadily increasing purchas ing power and consumption. But consump tion also requires time, and the large number of durable items of consumption also require time for maintenance. At the same time the improvements in productivity also mean that a steadily growing number of material goods can be produced per working hour. In other words, while the time available is con stant, the availability of material goods is on the increase. Thus in terms of consumption as well as production, in relative terms time is becoming a steadily scarcer and more ex pensive resource. A result of this is that it is becoming gradu ally more difficult to find room for time-con suming activities on one's schedule. Values linked with typically time-consuming activi ties such as caring for and being with child ren, family and friends, are given decreasing priority. The greatly reduced birth-rate in al most all of the industrialised countries may be a symptom of this. To the extent that continued develop ments in this direction may be regarded as prejudicing important societal functions and interests, controlling time utilisation might be an objective! One possible way of doing this could be by means of changes in normal working hours, calculated on a daily, weekly, annual or life-long basis. However, attempts at such control would not be particularly ef fective unless some form of wage compensa tion were granted to the person who reduced his/her working hours, or one somehow pre vented the time released from nevertheless being utilised to make extra money through the labour market. Against this view it may be argued that if certain types of activity gradually take up less of our time, this is because that is what people want. There is no reason why society should override the choice of the individual in this question, and any scheme that limits people's opportunities to choose between ti me and earnings contributes to reducing people's welfare. According to this view, the refore, time should be freely available for purchase on the labour market, by means of working hours' arrangements that are as flexible as possible and enable people to ma ke individual choices. 0.5 REGULATIONS GOVERNING WORKING HOURS Working hours are regulated by means of legislation and wage agreements. The inter est in regulating working hours has econo mic, social and welfare-related bases. The Working Environment Act regulates the central aspects of working hours: the length of daily and weekly working hours, overtime, work on Sundays and public holi days, night-work, breaks and time off, and the possibility of flexible working hours by utilising calculations of average working hours. As a point of departure the Act ap plies to any enterprise employing people, but it does not regulate the working hours of self employed people. Persons who hold leading positions or who do particularly indepen dent jobs are also exempt from the working hours' regulations in the Act. The same ap plies to certain branches and industries. The legislation only states the outer limits within which working hours must be kept. Many wage agreements give enterprises tighter frameworks for location of working hours than the legislation gives room for. Moreover, in connection with the length of working hours the Working Environment Act gives much more leeway than most wage agreements, because of its provisions re garding calculation of average working hours in the space of a longer time-span than a week. 0.6 WORKING HOURS AND EARNINGS - A LIFE CYCLE PROBLEM The choice between various types of work ing hours' reforms is among other things a question of when in life each individual should be able to grant him/herself more ti me for aims outside of work - in youth, old age, middle age or in equal portions through out the whole of a lifetime. On the Continent the reduc tion of unemployment has been the predo minant goal of working hours' reforms. In Scandinavia the debate on working hours has tended to concern itself more with welfa re and equal opportunities policies. To a varying degree the governments of the various countries have played an active role in connection with policy on working hours. France and Belgium are examples of count ries where the government has made consi derable efforts to divide work among more people and to «make room» for unemployed young people by introducing early retire ment. In other countries the conflict and debate surrounding working hours has first and fo remost been carried out by the employers' and employees' national organisations. This conflict has been particularly sharp in West Germany, where in spring 1984 the struggle surrounding the 35-hour week led to the most extensive wave of strikes in the history of the Federal Republic. For large groups this struggle ended in an agreement stipula ting a 38.5-hour working week. The question of wage compensation has been a major obstacle in the attempts to reach agreement on shorter working hours. The labour unions have claimed that full compensation is necessary among other things in order to maintain demand in socie ty and prevent fresh unemployment, whe reas the employers have claimed that wage compensation would increase the costs of en terprises, adversely affect profitability and competitiveness, and thus result in increa sed unemployment. In order to undo this knot, on the Conti nent discussion has increasingly centred on package deals where reduced working hours are combined with the increase or at any rate maintenance of capital utilisation time (ope rating hours). By detaching working hours from operating hours, the enterprises would be able to reduce capital costs per unit pro duced, thus «being able to afford» an increa se in hourly pay. At the same time it might be possible for the demand for labour to increase. However, such schemes have also met opposition from many employees' organisa tions, since extended operating hours would also necessarily involve more extensive use of shift work and/or inconvenient or reloca ted working hours. Another type of flexibility that has often been coupled with shortened working hours is schemes that make it possible to adapt working hours to seasonal fluctuations in de mand. In many countries the authorities ha ve tried to pave the way for such flexibility, by changing statutory regulations governing the length and location of working hours. The introduction of annual working hours or other arrangements that give longer calcula tion periods than one week have gradually become more common. However, in relation to the debate in Norway it is interesting to note that the new, liberal statutory regula tions governing calculation of average work ing hours in, for example, France and Bel gium do not provide significantly greater sco pe for flexibility than the Norwegian Work ing Environment Act. Both on the Continent and in Norway it is wage agreements and not legislation that first and foremost limit the length and location of working hours. However, lately the employees' organisa tions in a number of countries on the Conti nent have become less opposed to flexible arrangements, and gradually a number of agreements have been entered into which combine shorter average working hours with greater flexibility regarding the location of working hours. The change in attitude of the employees' organisations is partly due to fin dings that indicate that many employees pre fer or at any rate accept untraditional ar rangements regarding working hours. At least as important is recognition of the fact that labour organisations may gradually co me to lose their influence if more flexible schemes nevertheless force their way in without the participation and control of the employees' organisations. The conditions for accepting such forms of liberalisation would however still seem to be that employees should receive benefits in the form of shor ter working hours or perhaps higher wages.
